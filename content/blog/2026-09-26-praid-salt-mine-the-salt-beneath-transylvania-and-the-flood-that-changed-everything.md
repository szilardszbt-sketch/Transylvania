  Deep in the heart of Transylvania, in Romania's Harghita County, lies a place
  where the history of an

  entire community has been written beneath the ground.


  Praid is a small settlement in the historic Szeklerland, surrounded by forests, mountains and the valleys

  of the Eastern Carpathians. Beneath it lies one of Romania's most important rock-salt deposits — a vast

  geological formation that has been exploited for centuries.


  For generations, salt was more than a commodity here. It was wealth, employment, transport, trade

  and political power. Later, the same underground chambers that had been created to extract salt

  became something entirely different: a destination for visitors seeking the unusual microclimate of a

  salt mine, including people undergoing respiratory treatments.


  By the beginning of the 21st century, Praid had become much more than a mining community.

  Hundreds of thousands of tourists were travelling there every year. Hotels, guesthouses, restaurants,

  shops, transport operators and local attractions all benefited from the flow of visitors.


  Then, in May 2025, the relationship between Praid and its greatest natural asset was violently disrupted.


  After exceptionally heavy rainfall, the Corund stream rose dramatically. Water penetrated the mine,

  protective infrastructure was overwhelmed and eventually the underground workings were flooded.

  The disaster did not merely close a tourist attraction. It threatened an economic system upon which

  much of Praid had come to depend.


  More than a year later, the mine remains closed.


  And the question is no longer simply whether Praid will reopen. It is whether the old mine can ever

  return to the role it played before the flood — or whether Praid will have to build its future around

  something new.

***A History Written in Salt***

The story of Praid's salt begins long before the modern mine.

The salt deposit itself was formed millions of years ago, when ancient seas covered parts of what is now

Transylvania. Geological studies have described the Praid deposit as one of Europe's major rock-salt

accumulations. One scientific study estimated the geological reserve at around 3 billion tonnes of rock

salt. 

Human beings discovered its value much later.

There is an important distinction when discussing the "opening" of Praid.

Salt exploitation in the area is ancient, and a 1291 document is generally cited as the first written

record of salt exploitation at Praid. At that time, the workings were surface exploitations rather than

the underground industrial mine that exists in the modern historical record. 

The decisive date for modern mining is 1762.

That year, under the supervision of Austrian mining engineer Frendl Aladár, the József (Joseph) mine

was opened on the south-western side of the Salt Hill. This marked the beginning of organised

underground exploitation at Praid. Surface extraction continued alongside underground mining for

several years. 

By 1787, Praid had become property of the Treasury of Vienna and systematic state-controlled

exploitation was established. The mine subsequently developed through a succession of workings,

including the Parallel mine and the Elisabeth mine. 

The salt was not simply consumed locally.

For centuries it was an economically important resource throughout Transylvania and the Szekler

communities. Historical accounts describe salt from Praid being transported across a substantial

surrounding territory, while local inhabitants had particular rights connected with the salt trade. 

Salt was valuable enough to be controlled by rulers and governments. At different periods, the ability to

extract and sell it was treated as a privilege, while the state exercised increasing control over its

production and distribution.

This is one reason Praid's history cannot really be separated from the history of the wider region.

The salt was beneath Praid, but its economic influence extended far beyond the mine.

***From Hand Tools to Industrial Mining***

For much of its early history, extracting salt was brutally physical work.

Traditional mining techniques involved cutting into the salt and removing blocks manually. Horses,

winches and human labour played an important role in bringing the material to the surface.

The technology changed dramatically during the 20th century.

After the Second World War, explosives replaced the older manual extraction techniques. In 1947, the

Gheorghe Doja mine was opened using the newer mining approach, allowing much larger chambers to

be created. Later developments produced the enormous underground spaces that would eventually

make Praid famous among tourists. 

Production could be substantial.

![](/uploads/from-hand-tools-to-industrial-mining.jpeg)

Historical records show that Praid's output varied considerably according to economic conditions and

technology. For example, production reached approximately 136,000 tonnes in 1996, after having

fallen to around 70,000 tonnes in 1998. In 2003, production was approximately 122,500 tonnes. 

In the years immediately before the 2025 disaster, Praid's production capacity was generally reported at

approximately 70,000–100,000 tonnes of salt per year. 

There is, however, an important limitation when asking how much salt has been removed from Praid

throughout its entire history.

I could not find a reliable official source providing a single cumulative figure covering all centuries of

exploitation. The surviving historical records use different units, cover different periods and refer to

different workings. It would therefore be misleading to invent a grand total.

What can be said with confidence is that Praid has supplied salt continuously enough, and on a large

enough scale, to make the mine an important industrial asset for centuries.

And the extraordinary size of the remaining geological deposit explains why mining could continue for

so long.

***When the Mine Became More Than a Mine***

The most fascinating chapter in Praid's story began when miners discovered that the underground

environment itself could be valuable.

The huge chambers created by salt extraction offered a remarkably stable environment. The air was

exceptionally clean, humidity and temperature were relatively stable, and the concentration of airborne

particles was low.

During the 1960s, Praid began developing underground speleotherapy and climatotherapy in the

chambers of the Gheorghe Doja mine. The idea was to use the underground salt environment as a

therapeutic setting, particularly for people suffering from respiratory problems. 

This was the beginning of Praid's transformation.

The mine was still a working industrial operation, but part of the underground world was becoming a

place for treatment, relaxation and eventually tourism.

By around 1980, a dedicated therapy section had been developed around 120 metres below the surface.

What followed was unusual.

Instead of hiding the mine from the public, Praid increasingly invited people inside it.

Visitors descended underground and entered a world that looked nothing like an ordinary tourist

attraction: enormous salt chambers, underground roads, chapels, exhibition spaces, restaurants,

recreational areas, playgrounds and medical facilities.

The tourist base eventually occupied approximately 9,400 square metres, spread across seven former

exploitation chambers. 

There was even an underground chapel.

The mine became a place where industrial history, health tourism and leisure existed side by side.

For tourists, Praid offered something difficult to reproduce anywhere else.

You did not simply look at an old mine.

You entered it.

***The Salt Mine Becomes the Engine of Praid's Tourism Economy***

The economic importance of Praid eventually extended far beyond the workers employed directly by

Salrom, Romania's National Salt Company.

Every visitor arriving at the mine represented potential income for somebody else.

Guesthouses needed cleaners and reception staff.

Restaurants needed cooks and waiters.

Shops sold food, souvenirs and local products.

Tour buses and taxis transported visitors.

Local businesses sold traditional Szekler products.

The surrounding attractions benefited from people who came to Praid specifically because of the mine.

This created a classic tourism multiplier effect: the mine itself generated visitors, while the visitors

generated business for the wider community.

Before the 2025 disaster, the mine could attract around 500,000 visitors annually, with some reports

putting the figure even higher in particularly successful years. 

The importance of that number becomes clearer when considering the size of Praid.

This is not a major Romanian city with a diversified economy. It is a small community whose tourism

industry grew around a single extraordinary natural asset.

The mine had effectively become Praid's economic engine.

That is why the events of 2025 were so devastating.

***The Warning Before the Disaster***

The 2025 catastrophe did not happen in complete isolation.

Water infiltration had already been a problem.

In May 2024, heavy rainfall caused water to enter the mine. Salrom temporarily suspended production

and tourist visits while emergency works were carried out.

A bypass was constructed to divert the Corund stream, together with a protective channel designed to

handle larger flows. The mine was subsequently reopened to visitors on 15 June 2024. 

At the time, Salrom said the emergency works had made the mine safe for tourism and production,

while also acknowledging that larger long-term investments would be required. 

That detail would become extremely important one year later.

Because in 2025, the same river would overwhelm the system.

***May 2025: When the Water Came***

The first major shutdown came on 5 May 2025.

Following intense rainfall, the level of the Corund stream rose sharply. The water arrived carrying

sediment, debris, wood and other material, obstructing protective works around the mine.

Water began infiltrating the underground workings.

Salrom stopped both salt production and tourist access. 

Initially, the objective was to contain the situation.

Workers, water authorities and emergency services began constructing and reinforcing channels,

installing pumps and attempting to redirect the Corund stream.

Romania's National Administration "Apele Române" later said it mobilised more than 100 people and

substantial equipment, including high-capacity pumps, excavators, generators and lighting systems. 

For several weeks, the battle continued.

Then the weather turned the emergency into a disaster.

On 27 May, the flow of the Corund increased dramatically. Apele Române reported that the discharge

rose from approximately 6 cubic metres per second to around 60 cubic metres per second.

The flow overwhelmed emergency channels constructed to protect the mine. Debris carried by the flood

damaged the geomembrane used to waterproof the channels, while water reactivated pathways

through the salt massif. 

The water had found its way back into the mountain.

And salt has a fundamental weakness when confronted by large quantities of water:

water dissolves it.

As one Romanian water authority specialist explained during the crisis, when water comes into contact

with salt, it immediately begins dissolving it, creating cavities and potentially affecting structural

stability. 

The situation rapidly became critical.

***The Mine Floods***

By 29 May 2025, the underground cavity system had been flooded to approximately the level of the

Corund stream, according to subsequent hydrological research. Protective structures constructed

underground had been overwhelmed by the floodwater. 

The mine was no longer simply experiencing water infiltration.

It was flooded.

Salt stocks underground were compromised. Machinery and equipment could no longer be recovered.

The Telegdy mining sector was lost to the water. 

The consequences extended above ground.

Authorities became concerned about ground instability and possible collapses. At one point, evacuation

measures affected dozens of households in the area around the mine. Apele Române constructed a

defensive line using approximately 500 metres of metal panels and 14,000 sandbags to protect homes

from a potential flood wave caused by underground collapse. 

The problem also became an environmental emergency.

As salt dissolved and saline water entered the surrounding water system, salinity increased

downstream. Drinking-water supplies in parts of the region were affected, and authorities had to

monitor chloride concentrations in rivers including the Târnava Mică. 

The disaster therefore had three interconnected dimensions:

a mining disaster, an environmental emergency and an economic crisis.

***Could It Have Been Prevented?***

This remains one of the most sensitive questions surrounding Praid.

During and after the disaster, questions were raised about whether the mine had been adequately

protected despite the warning provided by the 2024 infiltration event.

Romania's Inspectorate of State Construction recorded that the immediate technical cause involved the

exceptional rise of the Corund stream, with the bypass constructed in 2024 unable to handle the

resulting flow. It also stated that the exact causes would have to be established through technical

expertise and a post-event investigation. 

The distinction is important.

It is established that the extraordinary rainfall and flood overwhelmed the protective system.

It is not yet appropriate to state as established fact that a particular institution or individual

caused the disaster through negligence.

Those questions require technical and administrative investigations.

The criticism has nevertheless remained politically significant. A year after the disaster, there were

renewed calls for a parliamentary investigation into whether the catastrophe could have been avoided. 

***The Economic Shock***

The mine's closure immediately hit Praid.

The economic impact was much greater than the loss of mining production.

Tourists stopped coming.

Guesthouses lost bookings.

Restaurants lost customers.

Small traders lost their principal source of seasonal income.

Transport operators lost passengers.

Local employees connected directly or indirectly to tourism were affected.

By April 2026, local authorities reported that 135 businesses had ceased activity during 2025, most of

them in Praid and neighbouring communities dependent upon tourism. The affected businesses

included small food and street traders as well as accommodation providers. 

The prefect of Harghita later described the consequences as extending beyond the economy into the

morale of the population.

One year after the flood, he said that neither the economic situation nor the morale of local residents

was good. 

That tells us something important about what the mine had become.

Praid had not merely built a tourism industry around the salt mine.

It had built part of its identity around it.

***Romania's Response***

The Romanian government responded with emergency financial support.

In 2025, the government approved approximately 300 million lei in support connected with the

recovery operation and affected businesses. Up to 200 local companies were also included in support

measures intended to cushion the economic consequences of the tourism collapse. 

Salrom subsequently reported that it submitted documentation for 76.5 million lei in compensation for

losses under the relevant state-support arrangements and received the full requested amount in

December 2025. 

European assistance was also requested.

Experts from several European countries, including Spain, Germany, the Netherlands and Hungary,

travelled to Praid to assess the mine and help determine possible solutions. 

The experts considered more than one possible future.

One option involved attempting to stabilise and save parts of the existing mine.

Another involved developing a new underground gallery for tourism and production, rather than

attempting to restore everything that had been lost. 

That distinction could ultimately determine Praid's future.

***So, Will Praid Salt Mine Ever Reopen?***

This is where the story becomes uncertain.

The simple answer is:

There is no confirmed date for reopening the historic tourist mine.

Salrom continues to describe Praid as temporarily closed because of the May 2025 flooding. Its current

public information still states that the mine remains closed. 

More importantly, authorities are still treating the area as an emergency zone.

As recently as August 2026, the local authorities extended the state of alert for Praid for another 30

days, through 6 September 2026. Public access to the affected area and the Salt Canyon remained

prohibited, while authorities continued monitoring the mine perimeter and the Corund stream. 

That is a very different situation from simply repairing a damaged tourist attraction.

The ground itself must be stabilised.

The water system must be controlled.

The risk of further dissolution and collapse must be assessed.

The river must be managed so that another major flood cannot simply repeat the disaster.

Only after those questions have been resolved can the possibility of safely returning tourists

underground be properly considered.

There is, however, reason to believe that the Romanian authorities have not abandoned the idea of

restoring Praid.

In July 2026, acting Environment Minister Diana Buzoianu said that funding had been allocated and that

the preliminary stages for the restoration project had been completed. She said construction work

could begin during 2026, with the objective of ensuring that a future flood could be diverted and would

not cause another catastrophe. 

That is significant.

But restoration work beginning is not the same thing as the historic tourist mine reopening.

At present, there is no official guarantee that visitors will once again descend into the same

underground galleries that existed before May 2025.

***Praid Without Its Mine***

The people of Praid have therefore been forced to confront a difficult question:

What happens when the attraction that created the local tourism industry disappears?

The answer has been to look beyond the mine.

Local authorities and entrepreneurs have been developing alternative tourism based on the landscape,

traditional culture, gastronomy, outdoor activities and other attractions in the wider Salt Country.

The area already possesses considerable assets.

There is the Salt Canyon.

There are hiking routes around the Salt Mountain.

Nearby Corund is famous for its traditional pottery.

Sovata has its famous Bear Lake and spa tradition.

The surrounding Szekler villages possess distinctive architecture, food, crafts and cultural traditions.

The closure has therefore created an uncomfortable opportunity: Praid may be forced to become a

destination rather than simply the place where tourists went to visit a salt mine.

Local authorities have explicitly been working on this diversification, describing it as an attempt to

develop sustainable tourism experiences beyond the mine. 

But replacing half a million visitors is not simple.

A guesthouse does not care whether its visitor came to see a salt mine, hike through a forest or eat

traditional food. It needs visitors.

For that reason, rebuilding the wider tourism economy may be just as important as rebuilding the mine

itself.

***A Mine, a Community and an Uncertain Future***

The history of Praid is unusually circular.

For centuries, the salt beneath the mountain created wealth.

Then industrial mining created enormous underground chambers.

Those chambers eventually became a place for medicine, recreation and tourism.

Tourism transformed the surrounding settlement.

And then the same geological environment that had made Praid prosperous became the centre of an

environmental and economic disaster.

The mine is still there.

The salt deposit has not disappeared.

But parts of the underground world that generations of miners created have been permanently altered

by water.

That is why the future of Praid is so difficult to predict.

There is no shortage of salt beneath the mountain. Geological studies estimate an extraordinary

reserve, measured in billions of tonnes. 

The problem is not whether Praid has enough salt.

The problem is whether the underground spaces can be made safe again.

And even if they can, the future mine may not look like the old one.

It could involve new galleries.

Different access routes.

New flood-control infrastructure.

Different tourist facilities.

Or perhaps a combination of restored and newly developed underground spaces.

What is certain is that Praid has entered a new chapter.

For hundreds of years, the people of this corner of Transylvania looked underground for their

livelihood.

For several decades, they also looked underground for tourists.

Today, they are waiting to discover whether that relationship can be rebuilt.

The mountain still contains its treasure.

But for now, the water has won.
