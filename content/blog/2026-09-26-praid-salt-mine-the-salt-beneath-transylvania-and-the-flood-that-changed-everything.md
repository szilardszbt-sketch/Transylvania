  Here, much of the story is beneath your feet.


  In Harghita County, in the historic Szeklerland, the small settlement of Praid sits among the forests, mountains and valleys of the Eastern Carpathians. Beneath it lies one of Romania's great rock-salt deposits, a geological formation created millions of years ago when an ancient sea covered parts of what is now Transylvania.


  For centuries, that salt meant wealth, work and survival. Generations of people made their living from it, transported it, traded it and fought to control it. Then something remarkable happened: the great underground spaces created by mining became a place for treatment, relaxation and eventually tourism.

  But Praid's story now has a much sadder chapter.


  In May 2025, after exceptionally heavy rainfall, the Corund stream rose dramatically. Water penetrated the mine, protective infrastructure was overwhelmed and the underground workings were flooded. What was lost was not simply a tourist attraction. A large part of the local economy had grown around that mine.


  More than a year later, Praid remains closed, and the future of the historic tourist mine is still uncertain.

## A History Written in Salt

The story begins millions of years before anyone thought of building a mine here.

An ancient sea once covered parts of the Transylvanian Basin. As that sea disappeared, enormous quantities of salt were left behind underground. Geological studies have described the Praid deposit as one of Europe's major rock-salt accumulations, with one study estimating the geological reserve at around 3 billion tonnes of rock salt.

It is difficult to imagine a number that large when you are standing on the surface. The mountain looks like part of the landscape, yet underneath it is an extraordinary quantity of salt that has shaped human history for centuries.

The earliest commonly cited written record of salt exploitation at Praid dates to 1291. At that time, salt was being extracted from surface workings rather than from the organised underground mine that we associate with Praid today.

The important date for modern underground mining is 1762.

That year, under the supervision of Austrian mining engineer Frendl Aladár, the József (Joseph) mine was opened on the south-western side of Salt Hill. This marked the beginning of organised underground exploitation. Surface extraction continued alongside the underground workings for several years.

By 1787, Praid had come under the Treasury of Vienna, and systematic state-controlled exploitation had been established. Other workings followed, including the Parallel and Elisabeth mines.

And the salt did not stay in Praid.

For centuries it was transported throughout a substantial part of Transylvania and was particularly important to the Szekler communities. Salt was valuable enough for rulers and governments to control its extraction, production and distribution.

When I think about that history, I find it striking that something as ordinary as a handful of salt could have carried such economic and political importance.

Today we take salt for granted. In earlier centuries, it could represent money, food preservation, employment and power.

## From Hand Tools to Industrial Mining

The early work must have been extremely hard.

Salt was cut and removed manually, with horses, winches and human labour playing important roles in getting it to the surface. The mine was not the comfortable visitor attraction people would later know. It was a workplace where extracting the salt demanded physical strength and endurance.

The technology changed dramatically during the 20th century.

After the Second World War, explosives replaced older manual extraction techniques. In 1947, the Gheorghe Doja mine was opened using this newer approach, allowing much larger underground chambers to be created.

Those enormous chambers would eventually become one of the reasons people travelled to Praid not to work underground, but to experience it.

That is one of the things I love about the story of Transylvania: places rarely have only one life. A building, a road, a mine or a village can change its purpose while carrying its earlier history with it.

Praid's underground world did exactly that.

## When the Mine Became More Than a Mine

The really unusual chapter began when people realised that the underground environment itself had value.

Deep underground, the air was exceptionally clean, humidity and temperature were relatively stable, and airborne particles were low. During the 1960s, Praid began developing speleotherapy and climatotherapy in the chambers of the Gheorghe Doja mine.

The idea was to use the underground salt environment as a therapeutic setting, particularly for people with respiratory problems.

By around 1980, a dedicated therapy section had been developed approximately 120 metres below the surface.

Imagine descending into a working salt mine and finding yourself somewhere designed not for miners, but for people seeking treatment and cleaner underground air.

From there, the transformation continued.

Praid increasingly opened its underground world to visitors. People were no longer simply going underground because their jobs demanded it. They were going there because they wanted to see what was beneath the mountain.

And there was plenty to see.

The visitor area eventually covered approximately 9,400 square metres, spread across seven former exploitation chambers. There were underground roads, chapels, exhibition spaces, restaurants, recreational areas, playgrounds and medical facilities.

There was even an underground chapel.

That mixture still amazes me. You could be standing in a place created by generations of miners, surrounded by salt walls, and then find yourself somewhere designed for worship, treatment, leisure or simply spending an afternoon with your family.

The mine had become a world beneath the world above.

## The Mine Becomes the Engine of Praid

Eventually, the influence of the mine reached far beyond the underground workings.

Salrom, Romania's National Salt Company, employed people directly, but thousands of others benefited indirectly from the visitors who arrived in Praid.

Guesthouses needed cleaners and reception staff. Restaurants needed cooks and waiters. Shops sold food, souvenirs and local products. Taxis and tour buses carried visitors. Local businesses sold traditional Szekler products.

A visitor coming to Praid for the mine might sleep in a local guesthouse, eat in a restaurant, buy something from a shop and visit another attraction nearby.

The mine therefore became much more than an attraction. It became the economic engine of the settlement.

Before the 2025 disaster, it could attract around 500,000 visitors a year, with some reports putting the figure even higher in particularly successful years.

For a small community like Praid, that is enormous.

And that is what makes what happened next so painful.

## There Was a Warning

The flood of 2025 did not come completely without warning.

In May 2024, heavy rainfall had already caused water to enter the mine. Salrom temporarily suspended both production and tourist visits while emergency works were carried out.

A bypass was constructed to divert the Corund stream, together with a protective channel designed to handle larger flows. The mine eventually reopened to visitors on 15 June 2024.

At the time, Salrom said the emergency works had made the mine safe for tourism and production, while also acknowledging that larger long-term investments would be needed.

One year later, that detail would become painfully important.

## May 2025: When the Water Came

The first major shutdown came on 5 May 2025.

After intense rainfall, the Corund stream rose sharply. The water brought sediment, debris, wood and other material with it, obstructing protective works around the mine.

Then the water began getting into the underground workings.

Production stopped. Tourist access stopped.

Workers, water authorities and emergency services began fighting to contain the situation. Channels were constructed and reinforced. Pumps were installed. Attempts were made to redirect the Corund stream.

Romania's National Administration "Apele Române" eventually mobilised more than 100 people, together with high-capacity pumps, excavators, generators and lighting equipment.

For several weeks, people fought to keep the water out.

Then, on 27 May, everything changed.

The flow of the Corund increased from approximately 6 cubic metres per second to around 60 cubic metres per second.

Ten times the earlier flow.

The emergency channels could not cope. Flood debris damaged the waterproofing geomembrane, while water reactivated pathways through the salt massif.

The water had found its way back into the mountain.

And this is where the situation became particularly frightening.

Salt and water do not make a forgiving combination.

Water dissolves salt. As water enters a salt deposit, it can create cavities and potentially affect the stability of the surrounding structure.

By 29 May 2025, the underground cavity system had been flooded to approximately the level of the Corund stream.

The mine was no longer dealing with an infiltration problem.

**It was flooded.**

## The Moment Praid Changed

This is the part of the story that I find hardest to read about.

The underground spaces that generations of miners had created were suddenly being filled with floodwater. Salt stocks were compromised. Machinery and equipment could no longer be recovered. The Telegdy mining sector was lost to the water.

Above ground, people were also frightened.

Authorities became concerned about ground instability and possible collapses. Evacuation measures affected dozens of households in the area around the mine.

A defensive line approximately 500 metres long, using metal panels and around 14,000 sandbags, was constructed to protect homes from a potential flood wave caused by an underground collapse.

At that point, this was no longer simply a mining disaster.

It had become an environmental emergency and a threat to people's homes.

As salt dissolved and saline water entered surrounding waterways, salinity increased downstream. Drinking-water supplies in parts of the region were affected, and authorities monitored chloride concentrations in rivers including the Târnava Mică.

When I think of Praid now, I cannot separate the image of that underground world from the people above it. For visitors, the mine had been an extraordinary place to explore. For local people, it was part of their livelihoods and their community.

Suddenly, they had to worry about whether the mountain beneath them was stable.

## Could It Have Been Prevented?

This remains one of the most sensitive questions surrounding the disaster.

It is important to distinguish between what has been established and what remains under investigation.

Romania's Inspectorate of State Construction recorded that the immediate technical cause involved the exceptional rise of the Corund stream and that the bypass constructed in 2024 was unable to handle the resulting flow.

However, the precise causes require technical expertise and post-event investigation.

So I would be careful about simply saying that somebody "caused" the disaster or that negligence has been established. The evidence in the source material does not support making that conclusion as a fact.

There have nevertheless been continuing political questions about whether the catastrophe could have been avoided, including renewed calls for a parliamentary investigation a year after the flood.

## The Economic Shock

Once the mine closed, the consequences spread rapidly through Praid.

Tourists stopped arriving.

Guesthouses lost bookings. Restaurants lost customers. Small traders lost seasonal income. Transport operators lost passengers. People whose jobs were connected directly or indirectly to tourism were affected.

By April 2026, local authorities reported that 135 businesses had ceased activity during 2025, most of them in Praid and neighbouring communities dependent upon tourism.

That number gives the economic story a human face.

Behind every closed guesthouse or food stall is somebody's income. Behind every empty restaurant is somebody wondering what comes next.

The prefect of Harghita later described the consequences as extending beyond the economy into the morale of the local population, saying one year after the flood that neither the economic situation nor the morale of local residents was good.

That makes sense to me.

Praid had spent decades building an identity around its salt mine. People had grown accustomed to visitors arriving, restaurants filling, guesthouses operating and the local economy moving with the rhythm of tourism.

Then, almost overnight, the visitors disappeared.

## Romania's Response

The Romanian government responded with emergency financial support.

In 2025, approximately 300 million lei was approved in support connected with the recovery operation and affected businesses. Up to 200 local companies were included in support measures intended to cushion the economic consequences of the collapse in tourism.

Salrom subsequently reported submitting documentation for 76.5 million lei in compensation for losses under the relevant state-support arrangements and receiving the full requested amount in December 2025.

European assistance was also requested.

Experts from Spain, Germany, the Netherlands and Hungary travelled to Praid to assess the situation and consider possible solutions.

And there was an important realisation: saving Praid might not necessarily mean restoring everything exactly as it was.

One possibility involved stabilising and saving parts of the existing mine.

Another involved constructing a new underground gallery for tourism and production rather than trying to restore everything that had been lost.

That distinction could shape Praid's future.

## So, Will Praid Salt Mine Ever Reopen?

This is where I have to resist giving you a neat ending.

There isn't one yet.

There is no confirmed reopening date for the historic tourist mine.

Salrom continues to describe Praid as temporarily closed because of the May 2025 flooding, and its public information still states that the mine remains closed.

More importantly, this is not simply a matter of repairing a damaged tourist attraction.

The ground must be stabilised. The water system has to be controlled. The risk of further dissolution and collapse has to be assessed. The Corund stream needs to be managed so that another major flood cannot simply repeat the disaster.

Only when those questions have been addressed can visitors safely be considered for a return underground.

There is, however, evidence that restoration has not been abandoned.

In July 2026, acting Environment Minister Diana Buzoianu said funding had been allocated and preliminary stages of the restoration project had been completed. She said construction work could begin during 2026, with the objective of ensuring that a future flood could be diverted and would not cause another catastrophe.

That is encouraging, but it is not the same as announcing that the old tourist mine will reopen.

There is currently no official guarantee that visitors will once again descend into exactly the same galleries that existed before May 2025.

## Praid Without Its Mine

Perhaps the saddest part of this story is that Praid cannot simply wait for the mine to return.

The community has had to start looking outward.

The surrounding area already has plenty to offer: the Salt Canyon, hiking routes around Salt Mountain, traditional pottery in nearby Corund, Bear Lake and the spa tradition of Sovata, as well as the architecture, food, crafts and traditions of the surrounding Szekler villages.

Local authorities and entrepreneurs have therefore been developing alternative tourism based on landscape, traditional culture, gastronomy and outdoor activities.

In a strange way, the closure has forced Praid to ask a question it probably should never have needed to ask:

**What is Praid when the salt mine is no longer its centre?**

Perhaps that is the new chapter beginning here.

The mine brought the visitors, but the wider region has always had more to offer. The challenge is turning those other attractions into an economy capable of supporting the people who live here.

And replacing something that attracted around half a million visitors a year will not be easy.
