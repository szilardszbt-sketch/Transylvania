If you speak a little Italian, Spanish or French, Romanian can give you a strange feeling of recognition. You may hear a word and think, *I know that one.*  Then the next sentence arrives and suddenly you are reminded that Romanian has spent nearly two thousand years travelling on a very different road from its Western Romance cousins. 

That is what makes Romanian so fascinating.

It is a Romance language — descended from Latin — but it developed in the eastern Balkans, far away from the great Romance-speaking centres of Western Europe.Over centuries, it absorbed influences from Slavic languages, Greek, Hungarian, Turkish and other neighbours, while retaining a remarkably strong Latin foundation.

So how did this happen?

**From Rome to Romania**

The story begins with the expansion of the Roman Empire.

Rome conquered Dacia under Emperor Trajan in AD 106, establishing the Roman province of Dacia north of the Danube. Latin became an important language of administration, the army, trade and urban life. But Romanian did not simply appear because Romans arrived and started speaking Classical Latin. 


Like Italian, French, Spanish and Portuguese, Romanian ultimately developed from the everyday varieties of spoken Latin — what linguists generally call Vulgar Latin.Over generations, this Latin changed locally, influenced by the languages spoken by the populations who adopted it.

 
Exactly how Romanian developed during the centuries after Roman rule is still one of the more debated subjects in Balkan historical linguistics. There is a particularly large gap in the written record: the earliest surviving Romanian texts date from the 16th century, more than a thousand years after the Roman presence in the region.

\
This missing period is important. Romanian was already a developed language by the time we can see it in writing.

 
**A Romance language surrounded by Slavic languages**

 Geography changed Romanian profoundly. 


Unlike French, Spanish or Italian, Romanian was separated from the other major Romance languages by large areas where Slavic, Hungarian and other languages were spoken. Meanwhile, Slavic-speaking populations became increasingly important throughout the Balkans.

 
The result was a language with a distinctly Latin core but considerable Balkan and Slavic influence. 

![](/uploads/word-comparisons.jpeg)

Take a simple comparison:

Romanian: **bun** — good

Italian: **buono** 

Spanish: **bueno**

French: **bon** 

The family resemblance is obvious. 

But Romanian also developed features that make it unusual among Romance languages. One of the most famous is its definite article, which is normally attached to the end of the noun rather than placed before it. 

For example: 

**om** = man 

**omul** = the man 

That little **\-ul** is doing the job that *the* does in English. 

Romanian also developed grammatical features shared with neighbouring Balkan languages. Linguists therefore often discuss Romanian as part of the **Balkan linguistic area**, not simply as a Romance language isolated from everything around it.  

**Romanian is not simply “Latin with Slavic words”**

It is tempting to describe Romanian as Latin mixed with Slavic vocabulary. That is too simple. 

Its basic grammatical structure remains fundamentally Romance, and a large part of its core vocabulary comes from Latin. At the same time, Romanian has borrowed words from the languages with which its speakers came into contact. 

There was significant Slavic influence, particularly during the medieval period. Later, Romanian absorbed words from Greek, Turkish and Hungarian, while the 19th century brought a strong wave of French influence. 

This last period is particularly interesting. 

As Romanian society modernised and developed stronger cultural connections with Western Europe, French became highly influential among educated Romanians. Many modern Romanian words connected with administration, politics, culture and modern life entered or were reinforced through French. 

So Romanian vocabulary tells a story of geography and history: Latin roots, Balkan surroundings, Slavic contact, Ottoman influence and eventually a strong cultural turn towards France.

**One language, several branches**

Romanian is not confined neatly within the modern borders of Romania.

The main variety is usually called **Daco-Romanian**. This is the Romanian spoken in Romania and the Republic of Moldova, as well as by communities in neighbouring countries. 

But historically, linguists recognise several Eastern Romance varieties: 

•  **Daco-Romanian** — the main variety spoken in Romania and Moldova 

•  **Aromanian** — traditionally spoken across parts of Greece, Albania, Bulgaria and North Macedonia 

•  **Megleno-Romanian** — spoken by a much smaller community in the southern Balkans 

•  **Istro-Romanian** — historically associated with the Istrian Peninsula in Croatia 

These varieties developed in different parts of the Balkans and were separated from one another over long periods. Modern linguists do not always agree on whether the southern varieties should be called Romanian dialects or separate languages, which is why you may find different classifications in different sources.  

Research by the Romanian Academy's Institute of Linguistics continues to examine these varieties across Romania, Moldova, Ukraine, Serbia, Hungary, Albania, Bulgaria, North Macedonia, Greece and Croatia.

**What about Moldova?**

This is where language and history become particularly interesting. 

Romanian is the official language of the Republic of Moldova. In 2023, Moldova amended its legislation so that the constitutional wording identifies **Romanian** as the state language.  

The Romanian spoken in Moldova belongs to the Daco-Romanian variety and is closely related to the Romanian spoken across the border. There are regional differences, vocabulary differences and, historically, a major difference in writing systems and political terminology, but linguistically the two varieties are closely connected. Oxford's linguistic research describes Romanian varieties spoken in Romania, Moldova and adjacent regions as largely mutually intelligible.

**Where else can you hear Romanian?**

Romanian has a much wider geographical footprint than a map of Romania suggests. 

It is, of course, the official language of Romania and Moldova. Romanian-speaking communities are also found in countries including Serbia, Ukraine and Hungary, while Romanian has a particularly established presence in Serbia's autonomous province of Vojvodina. Romanian is officially used there alongside several other minority languages, and Romanian-language education and media continue to operate in the region.  

Romanian speakers can also be found much further afield today, particularly because of modern migration from Romania and Moldova. 

That means Romanian is no longer simply a language of the Carpathians, the Danube and the Moldavian plains. You can hear it in communities across Europe and beyond.

**A language that carries a map inside it**

Perhaps the most interesting thing about Romanian is that you can almost read the history of Eastern Europe through the language itself. 

Its Latin foundations point back to Rome. 

Its Slavic influences reflect centuries of contact across the Balkans. 

Its Hungarian, Greek and Turkish elements reveal neighbouring cultures and political worlds. Its French vocabulary tells the story of Romania's 19th-century modernisation. 

And its survival across the Danube, the Carpathians and the borders of several modern states shows that languages do not always respect political maps. 

For me, that is the best way to understand Romanian. 

It is not simply a Romance language that happens to be spoken in Eastern Europe. It is the result of a remarkable journey: **Latin carried eastwards, transformed by the Balkans, and still spoken today by millions of people.** 

And once you know that story, Romanian place names, people's names and even everyday words start to look rather different. 

You are no longer just hearing a language. 

You are hearing nearly two thousand years of history.
